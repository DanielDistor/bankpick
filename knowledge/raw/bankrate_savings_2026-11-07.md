##  Editorial Integrity 

Bankrate's editorial policy is built on a simple premise: ordinary people deserve the same financial information the institutions have. Our [editorial guidelines](https://www.bankrate.com/editorial-guidelines/ "editorial guidelines") govern what we cover, how we cover it, and who influences it, so you never have to wonder whether a recommendation was earned. 

###  Key principles 

We value your trust, so every editorial decision starts with one question: will this help the person reading it? Rather than just telling you our content is credible, we show you why it is. We trace every claim back to a source, and we stand behind every finding we publish. But naming a problem isn't enough on its own, so every story also gives you a way to act on what you've learned. 

###  Editorial independence 

Bankrate's editorial team writes on behalf of you, the reader. Our job is to give you the best advice we can, so you can make smart decisions about your money. Advertisers have no say in that work. A firewall separates them from the editorial side, and no editor or reporter here receives direct compensation from an advertiser.

##  Rate trends for savings accounts 

Savings account rates have been declining in recent years — in response to six Federal Reserve rate cuts in 2024 and 2025. The Fed kept rates steady in 2026 up until the September meeting, when it raised rates to 3.75% to 4.00%. In the coming months, we may see APYs inch up in turn.

But even some competitive rates may not be beating inflation anymore. The Consumer Price Index (CPI; a common and closely followed gauge of inflation) was reported as 3.4% on an annual basis for August — up significantly from 2.4% for February. That means if your savings APY is earning less than that, you are technically losing some purchasing power.

It's not just inflation working against savers — it's where they keep their money. Many of the nation's largest brick-and-mortar banks pay just 0.01% APY on their standard savings accounts. That translates to only $1 in interest per year on a $10,000 balance. Meanwhile, online-only banks — with far lower overhead than big banks with branches — sometimes offer APYs up to 400 times greater.

Whether or not your APY is outpacing inflation, keeping your money in a bank that’s paying a competitive rate helps you grow your savings more quickly.

  - You're saving for a major medium- or short-term goal, such as a vacation, wedding or home down payment.

##  How to choose a high-yield savings account 

When shopping around for the right account, take a look at Bankrate’s [expert reviews of popular banks](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/reviews/), many of which offer competitive rates. Also consider these important features when [choosing the best high-yield savings account](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/how-to-choose-the-best-high-yield-savings-account/):

  1. ###  A competitive yield 

Your savings will grow more quickly when you're earning higher interest. It's easy to assume your money is earning something reasonable if it's parked at a big, familiar bank — but these often pay just 0.01% APY on standard savings, well below the national average of 0.57%. Meanwhile, competitive accounts are earning up to around 400 times what the biggest banks pay. The difference between a high APY and a near-zero one could add up to hundreds, if not thousands, in interest over time.

  2. ###  No monthly service fee 

Rather than letting a monthly service fee eat into your savings, find an account that won’t charge this pesky fee. The options on Bankrate’s list typically don’t charge such a fee.

  3. ###  Access to your money when you need it 

Being able to withdraw your money easily is important, especially in an emergency. You may be able to take money out of your savings account at an ATM, using a peer-to-peer money transfer app or through an ACH transfer. You may also want to seek banks that allow unlimited withdrawals each month, rather than restricting you to six.

##  Ways to use a high-yield savings account 

A high-yield savings account provides easy access to your cash, making it a good place to earn some interest while you [build up an emergency fund](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/savings/starting-an-emergency-fund/) or save for goals such as a wedding or [down payment on a house](https://www.bankrate.com/mortgages/how-to-save-for-a-down-payment/). While you’re adding to your savings, consider using the interest you earn to pay for discretionary expenses such as a streaming service or a gym membership.

If you’re saving for multiple goals in a single account, it can pay to choose an account that allows you to separate the funds into categories. This way, it’s easy to track how much you have saved for each goal. For instance, [Ally Bank](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/reviews/ally-bank/) lets you create up to 30 savings "buckets."

One way to grow your nest egg is by setting up recurring [automatic transfers](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/savings/grow-your-savings-with-automatic-transfers/). Moving a set amount from your checking to your savings every payday helps ensure you’ll save consistently and reach your goals.

  - ###  Why do big banks pay less interest than online banks?

Caret Down Icon

Large brick-and-mortar banks carry the cost of maintaining branch networks, ATMs and in-person staff — expenses online-only banks don't have. Many big banks also count on inertia; customers who've banked there for years and don't shop around tend to stay, regardless of the rate. Online banks, by contrast, compete almost entirely on rate and low fees to win new customers, which is why they can afford to pay several times the national average.

  - ###  Should I get a CD instead of a high-yield savings account?

Caret Down Icon

CDs generally earn a fixed APY for a set period of time. In exchange for the guaranteed return, you agree to leave your money locked away for the chosen term. With high-yield savings accounts, you deal with variable APYs in exchange for being able to withdraw your money at any time. This makes a high-yield savings account a better place for your emergency fund or other money you may need in the near term.
